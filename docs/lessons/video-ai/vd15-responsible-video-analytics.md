## Why this module comes last and is not optional

Everything before this module made a system that works. This module decides whether it should
exist in the form it has. Video analytics is the branch of machine learning with the shortest
path from "technically possible" to "harmful at scale", because the input is people in public
space and the output is a persistent, searchable description of them.

The section's own position, stated as an engineering commitment rather than a disclaimer:

| We do | We do not |
| --- | --- |
| Use synthetic video generated by this course (CC0, no people) | Ship footage of identifiable people |
| Model four vehicle classes and two garment attributes | Infer identity, gender, age, ethnicity, health |
| Count crossings and aggregate flow | Track individuals across cameras |
| Report measured failure rates | Claim accuracy we have not measured |
| Delete generated frames after the lab | Retain appearance descriptors |

Those commitments are structural: `GarmentNet` has exactly two heads, so it *cannot* output an
identity; the ground truth has no person labels; the data generator draws rectangles.

## Data: provenance, consent, and licence

Before any model, answer four questions in writing:

1. **Where did the footage come from?** Your own recording, a licensed dataset, a public dataset
   under a specific licence, or synthetic. "Found online" is not an answer.
2. **Do you have the right to process it for this purpose?** A dataset licensed for research is
   not automatically licensed for a commercial product; a public webcam is not automatically
   lawful to record.
3. **Were the people in it informed?** Consent, clear notice, or a legal basis that does not
   require consent. In the EU/UK, GDPR Art. 6 provides the bases and Art. 9 constrains special
   categories; a workplace camera recording employees is a different legal situation from a
   public road.
4. **How long will you keep it, and who can access it?** Data minimisation and storage limitation
   are requirements, not best practice.

This section's data card answers all four, and it is short because the answers are simple:

```
source      : generated by scripts/make_video_data.py (video_core.synth)
content     : rectangles on a synthetic road; no people, no real location
licence     : CC0-1.0 (dedicated to the public domain)
retention   : regenerable in ~2 s; data/video/ is git-ignored and safe to delete
access      : whoever has the repository
```

Substituting real footage means writing your own card with real answers. Lab 1 and Lab 2 ask you
to do exactly that if you use your own clip.

## Privacy by design, in code

| Principle | Implementation | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimisation | only boxes, classes, counts; no frames retained by default | Labs 3, 7 |
| Purpose limitation | no identity field anywhere in the schema | `video_core.labels` |
| Storage limitation | generated frames are disposable; outputs are small JSON | `outputs/` |
| Access control | run locally; no upload path in any lab | all labs |
| Transparency | every report names its data and its limitations | `report.py` |
| Accuracy | measured failure rates, not claims | Labs 2-8 |

Two design choices are worth copying because they remove whole classes of risk:

- **Aggregate, do not identify.** A count of trucks per hour is not personal data. A list of
  number plates is. The section's pipeline stops at aggregates, and that is a decision made at the
  schema level - there is no plate field to accidentally populate.
- **Do not store what you do not need.** The labs write annotated videos for review; those are
  outputs you delete, not a corpus you keep. If your system stores crops for "later analysis", you
  have created a face database by accident.

## Is this system surveillance?

A line counter on a public road is traffic engineering. The same code pointed at a building
entrance, with per-person IDs retained, is something else. The technical work is identical; the
difference is purpose, scope, retention, and who can query it. Four questions to ask before
building:

1. **Who benefits, and who is exposed?** A city counting vehicles to time a traffic light versus
   an employer counting how long staff take on breaks.
2. **Is the data aggregated or individual?** Can the output be joined to a person later?
3. **Is there a less invasive way?** Inductive loop counters, radar, or manual counts often
   answer the same question with no video at all.
4. **Who can see the output, and for how long?** An audit log and a deletion date are part of the
   design, not an afterthought.

If your project's honest answer to (1) is "it exposes people who did not agree", stop and
redesign. This course will not help you build covert tracking, and no amount of accuracy makes it
acceptable.

## Fairness: what can go wrong without a protected attribute

This section never models a protected attribute, and its system can still be unfair. Three
mechanisms, all measurable:

| Mechanism | How it shows up | How you would detect it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Condition-dependent accuracy** | recall 0.897 in daylight, 0.044 at night | per-condition metrics (Lab 4) |
| **Size/scale bias** | small/distant vehicles missed; large ones detected | per-box-size recall |
| **Class prior bias** | every vehicle called a truck (73 of 73) | class histogram (Lab 2) |

The fairness question that follows: **who is affected by the errors?** A night-time undercount
in a residential area where cyclists and pedestrians share the road is not a neutral technical
deficit - it produces a traffic policy that ignores the people most at risk. The measured night
recall (0.044 for the fine-tuned model) is a fairness fact as well as an accuracy fact.

Report disaggregated metrics whenever your data has conditions you can name - day/night,
near/far, dense/sparse. If a group is not represented in your data, you cannot measure your
error on it, and you must say so rather than implying uniform performance.

## Governance: what a deployable system needs

| Artefact | Purpose | Where in this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Model card** | architecture, data, metrics, limits, intended use | [model-card.md](model-card.md) |
| **Data card** | provenance, licence, consent, retention | `data/video/dataset_card.md` |
| **Governance statement** | purpose, legal basis, access, retention, review | [governance.md](governance.md) |
| **Audit log** | who ran what, when, on which footage | Lab 7's summary + exit codes |
| **Incident path** | what happens when the system is wrong in public | your report's "limitations" |

The model card is the one artefact that reviewers actually read. Its most important section is
not the metrics - it is **"out of scope"**, where you say what the system must not be used for.
A model card without that section is marketing.

## The report paragraph you should be able to write

> "This system counts vehicles crossing a line in a fixed camera view. It was evaluated on seven
> synthetic clips; on held-out clips it counts the clean scene exactly and undercounts congested
> footage by 3 of 8 vehicles. It has no identity output and stores only aggregate counts and a
> review video that is deleted after inspection. It is not suitable for identifying people,
> inferring protected attributes, or operating at night without further data collection. Night
> recall is 0.044, which is the largest known gap."

Every sentence is a claim you can defend with a number from this section. That is the standard:
not "we considered ethics", but "here is what it does, here is what it cannot do, here is the
number".

## Checklist before you call a video system finished

- [ ] Data provenance, licence, and consent recorded in a data card.
- [ ] No identity field in the schema; no path that could produce one.
- [ ] Retention stated, and implemented (what gets deleted, when).
- [ ] Access control stated, and it is not "anyone with the link".
- [ ] Metrics disaggregated by the conditions you can name.
- [ ] The weakest measured number is in the report, in the summary, not a footnote.
- [ ] Out-of-scope uses listed explicitly.
- [ ] A person is accountable, named in the governance statement.
